# Discrete Fourier transform

Source: [Lecture 4, pp. 8–18, 24](../../raw/signals_4.pdf). The discrete
Fourier transform (DFT) maps a finite block of $N$ sequence values to $N$
frequency samples. In the notes, both the block and its DFT are treated as
periodic with period $N$.

## Definition

For one length-$N$ block,

$$X[n]=\sum_{k=0}^{N-1}x[k]e^{-j2\pi nk/N},\qquad
x[k]=\frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1}X[n]e^{j2\pi nk/N}.$$

The transform is discrete in time and frequency. The DFT is also the DTFT of
the finite block sampled at $N$ equally spaced frequencies
$\Omega_n=2\pi n/N$ (pp. 8, 10).

## Periodic and cyclic interpretation

The notes use periodic extensions of both the block and its DFT. A shift
within the block is therefore a **cyclic shift**: indices wrap modulo $N$.
The DFT turns cyclic convolution of two length-$N$ blocks into
multiplication of their DFTs; multiplication of the blocks corresponds to
periodic convolution in the DFT domain with a factor $1/N$ (pp. 9, 14).

For ordinary linear convolution of sequences with lengths $N_1$ and $N_2$,
the notes state that zero-padding to a transform length

$$N\ge N_1+N_2-1$$

is sufficient to avoid the circular wraparound in the retained convolution
result (p. 13).

## Zero-padding and frequency interpolation

Appending zeros to a finite sequence before taking its DFT samples its DTFT
more densely: the notes describe zero-padding in time as interpolation in
frequency. Conversely, zero-padding in frequency corresponds to interpolation
in time (p. 10). It adds samples to the transform representation, not new
measurements of the original signal.

## Worked example: cosine sequence

The notes use $x[k]=\cos(2\pi k/6)$ and an $N=6$ DFT (pp. 17–18). Using
$\cos\theta=(e^{j\theta}+e^{-j\theta})/2$ and the finite exponential-sum
identity gives

$$X[n]=
\begin{cases}
3, & n\equiv 1 \pmod 6 \text{ or } n\equiv -1 \pmod 6,\\
0, & \text{otherwise}.
\end{cases}$$

Within the six DFT bins, the nonzero values are $X[1]=X[5]=3$. This matches
the notes' statement that the periodically extended DFT has nonzero values
at indices $n=6\ell\pm1$ (p. 18).

## Fast Fourier transform

The final page of the DFT material introduces the fast Fourier transform
(FFT) by splitting an $N$-point DFT into two subsequences of length $N/2$
(p. 24). The notes do not give the full algorithm there.
